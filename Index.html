A working sheet for department chairs & program assessment

Reading a mastery score

A Canvas outcome number is not the average grade on an assignment. Across a cohort it is a pass rate — the share of students who cleared the mastery line. Plan the measure, interpret what it tells you, then decide what changes.

Part 1

Planning the measure

Most trouble with outcome data traces back to a decision made — or skipped — before anything was built. Work down these gates in order. What you choose here decides what the number at the end can honestly say. Everything you enter compiles into a dated version record at the bottom you can save as PDF.

New to Canvas Outcomes? Start with Instructure's official setup guide — What are Outcomes? — then work through the gates below for the part their docs don't cover: reading the number correctly.

1 Name the outcome you're measuring

Write it as the capability a student should demonstrate — not the assignment name.

2 Where is this measured?

This decides which calculation method fits.

3 How will Canvas roll the scores up?

The calculation method is a decision about what mastery means. Pick it on purpose, record why, then freeze it.

4 Set the ruler

Define the scale and where the mastery line sits — this is a local decision. 80% is typical for graduate work, but an accreditor may set it elsewhere (ACEND, for example, defines nutrition mastery at 70%). Nothing above means anything until the bar has a height.

5 The integration check

Subskills measured in isolation do not automatically roll up to the whole. A student can clear every scaffolded piece and still fail to put them together. Make sure something is aligned to the culminating task itself.

6 Lock it in

Any change to the wording, scale, threshold, or method breaks year-over-year comparison. Pilot while you're still tuning; then freeze and date it. There's no database here — each PDF you save is your version record.

7 Where will the data actually live?

This decides whether results reach the Learning Mastery Gradebook at all.

Part 2

Interpreting what you got

A different skill from setting it up. Plug in real scores and watch what the method does — then generate reporting language you can lift into a report without misstating the finding.

One student, three methods

Enter a student's scores in the order they were earned. The same work can pass under one method and fail under another — that difference is a decision you made in Part 1, not a quirk.

The cohort number is a pass rate

Enter how many students were measured and how many cleared the mastery line. The bar shows who got over — not how high anyone jumped.

Say it correctly

Built from your entries above. It names the statistic as a pass rate, states the method and threshold, and keeps the individual score separate from the cohort figure.

Part 3

The action step

A number you don't act on isn't assessment. This is the third literacy: planning tells you what to measure, interpreting tells you what you found, and this tells you what changes because of it. It prints with your record.

Close the loop

Name what this result implies and the single change it drives. Assign it and set a date to look again, so the loop actually closes.

On the decaying-average math: this tool uses the standard model — most recent score at 65%, the mean of earlier scores at 35%. Canvas has fiddly edge cases in how the first one or two scores are handled, and Instructure has renamed and adjusted this method over time. The direction (recency-weighting) is reliable; confirm the exact corner arithmetic and current New Quizzes behavior in your own Canvas instance.
